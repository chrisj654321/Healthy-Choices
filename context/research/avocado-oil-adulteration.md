# Avocado-oil adulteration evidence review

**Status:** UPDATED WITH 2023-2026 EVIDENCE - research only; no scoring signal or in-app copy approved  
**Research date:** 2026-08-19  
**Required before any product, score, or marketing use:** Claude validation and independent Opus review.

## Scope and decision gate

This review asks a narrow question: what do citable sources show about the
authenticity and quality of avocado oil sold in the United States and Canada?
It does **not** determine whether a specific current bottle, brand, lot, or
food product is authentic. It also does not support treating every avocado-oil
product as adulterated.

Until the required review is complete, do not add an avocado-oil penalty,
displayed warning, or product-level inference in Food Expose.

## What is proven by accessible primary research

### 1. Green and Wang, 2020 - retail bottled avocado oils in the United States

**Source:** Green, H. S.; Wang, S. C. *First report on quality and purity
evaluations of avocado oil sold in the US.* *Food Control* 116 (2020) 107328.
[DOI: 10.1016/j.foodcont.2020.107328](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodcont.2020.107328) |
[open-access article PDF](https://agnetwest.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/06.16.2020-avocado-oil-research-u.c.-davis.pdf)

**What was tested**

- **22** bottled avocado-oil samples: 14 from six grocery stores and 8 from
  two online sources in the United States.
- The paper included oils labeled extra virgin, refined, and unspecified. It
  measured quality markers (free fatty acids, peroxide value, UV absorbance,
  vitamin E) and authenticity markers (fatty-acid, sterol, and
  triacylglycerol profiles).

**Exact findings**

- The authors confirmed **three of 22** samples as soybean oil at or near
  **100%**: **two** labeled "extra virgin" avocado oil and **one** labeled
  "pure" avocado oil. This is the clearest direct substitution finding in the
  study.
- The paper says that all samples were tested before their label expiration
  date and that the **majority** were oxidized before that date. Under the
  proposed peroxide-value limit discussed in the paper, all but R1, R3, and
  R5 exceeded the limit - **19 of 22** by arithmetic from those three named
  exceptions. This is an oxidation/quality result, **not** a count of
  adulterated samples.
- Three additional samples (R1, U4, U5) had profiles the authors said could
  reflect natural variation, processing, or possible high-oleic sunflower or
  safflower-oil adulteration. The authors did **not** confirm adulteration for
  those three. Do not present them as confirmed substitution.

**What this supports**

A 2020 convenience sample found confirmed near-total soybean-oil substitution
in 3 retail bottles and substantial quality/oxidation concerns. It supports
continued authenticity scrutiny; it does not establish a current market-wide
prevalence or identify all avocado oils as diluted.

**Key limits**

- Small, non-random, one-time sample collected in 2020.
- Results apply to the specific samples/lots, not permanent brand judgments.
- At the time, enforceable avocado-oil standards were still developing; the
  paper discusses proposed comparators.

### 2. Green and Wang, 2023 - private-label bottled avocado oils in the US and Canada

**Source:** Green, H. S.; Wang, S. C. *Purity and quality of private labelled
avocado oil.* *Food Control* 152 (2023) 109837.
[DOI: 10.1016/j.foodcont.2023.109837](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodcont.2023.109837)

**What was tested**

- **36** private-label bottled avocado-oil samples collected throughout the
  United States and Canada.
- **29** were refined and **7** unrefined. The study evaluated quality and
  purity against the current proposed standards used by the authors.

**Exact findings**

- **31%** of the 36 samples were classified as pure.
- **36%** were of the advertised quality.
- Of the **29 refined** samples, **3** met both quality and purity standards,
  **11** met quality standards, and **8** met the proposed purity standards.
- Of the **7 unrefined** samples, **3** met the proposed purity standards;
  the paper's highlights state that **one** extra-virgin sample met both
  quality and purity standards. Thus the paper identifies **4 of 36** samples
  meeting both measures.

**What this supports**

The later private-label sample again found a substantial quality and
authenticity problem under the study's proposed standards. It supports saying
that "a 2023 study found only 31% of 36 sampled private-label oils met its
purity criteria."

**What it does not support**

Do **not** turn "31% pure" into "69% were confirmed to be diluted with seed
oils." The study evaluates compositional purity against proposed standards;
that result is not a named-adulterant confirmation for every remaining sample.
It is also another study from the same UC Davis research program, so it is a
follow-up market sample, not an independent replication.

### 3. Lopez-Alvarez, Li, Vizgordiski, and Wang, 2026 - processed foods labeled with avocado oil

**Source:** Lopez-Alvarez, N.; Li, X.; Vizgordiski, B.; Wang, S. C.
*Authenticity of avocado and olive oils used as ingredients in commercially
processed foods.* *Applied Food Research* 6(2) (2026) 102389.
[DOI: 10.1016/j.afres.2026.102389](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.afres.2026.102389)

**What was tested**

- **74** commercial chips, mayonnaise products, and salad dressings purchased
  in 2025-2026 from California retail stores and online sources.
- Products had to declare avocado oil or olive oil as their sole edible oil;
  two lots per product were collected and analyzed in duplicate.
- This is a study of oil extracted from **processed foods**, not bottles of
  avocado oil sold directly to consumers.

**Exact findings**

- **48 of 54** avocado-oil-labeled products (**88.9%**) had compositional
  profiles inconsistent with authentic avocado-oil ranges. The paper's
  abstract rounds this as **89%**.
- The authors report patterns consistent with substitution or dilution by
  vegetable oils and describe their findings as suggesting possible economic
  adulteration. They do not chemically identify a specific hidden oil for
  every inconsistent product.
- The same study found **1 of 20** olive-oil-labeled products inconsistent
  with authentic olive-oil ranges. That comparison is included only to retain
  the paper's full context; it is not an avocado-oil result.

**What this supports**

For a defined 2025-2026 sample of processed products marketed with avocado
oil as the sole edible oil, the study found widespread compositional
inconsistency. It supports a future, carefully scoped claim about selected
processed foods labeled with avocado oil - not about retail avocado-oil bottles
or every product that lists avocado oil alongside other oils.

**Key limits**

- California/online samples, a one-time collection, and two lots per product.
- The study reports compositional inconsistency; it does not establish intent
  or identify a specific adulterant for every product.
- It shares a senior author with the 2020 and 2023 studies, so it is not an
  independent replication of the UC Davis research program.

## Additional recent peer-reviewed evidence (2023-2026)

### 4. Green and Wang, 2023 - 25 additional commercial oils tested with TAG-PCA

**Source:** Green, H. S.; Wang, S. C. *Tandem Triacylglycerol (TAG) and PCA
Adulteration Detection Approach for Avocado Oil.* *Food Analytical Methods*
16 (2023) 850-856.
[DOI: 10.1007/s12161-023-02468-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12161-023-02468-7) |
[open-access article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12161-023-02468-7)

**What was tested**

- A reference cluster made from **68** authentic laboratory-produced avocado
  oils covering natural variation, plus common potential adulterant oils.
- **Three** producer-supplied oils of known origin and an additional **25**
  commercial oils of unknown purity purchased from different U.S. regions.
- The commercial results from the TAG-PCA screening method were checked using
  conventional fatty-acid and sterol analyses.

**Exact findings**

- **21 of 25** commercial samples were classified as not pure by TAG-PCA, and
  the authors report that all 21 classifications were corroborated by the
  fatty-acid and sterol results.
- Three samples fell within the authentic avocado-oil cluster. A fourth fell
  just outside it but was confirmed as pure by fatty-acid and sterol testing.
- Several non-pure samples clustered near high-oleic safflower or sunflower
  oil. Those locations support possible adulterant patterns, but the paper's
  approximate mixture estimates are not direct quantification of every hidden
  oil.

**Limits and independence**

This is a real additional U.S. market sample, but it came from the same UC
Davis research group as the 2020 and private-label 2023 studies. The paper
does not disclose brand names or establish that the 25 samples represent the
national market. Its **21/25** result must not be pooled with the other UC
Davis studies as if all samples were independent and randomly selected.

### 5. Marin-Obispo et al., 2025 - independent commercial-market study

**Source:** Marin-Obispo, L. M.; Mayorga-Martinez, A. A.;
Obispo-Fortunato, D. J.; et al. *Fatty acids and fatty alcohol esters as novel
markers of authenticity and extraction method of commercial avocado oil.*
*Food Chemistry: X* 27 (2025) 102451.
[DOI: 10.1016/j.fochx.2025.102451](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fochx.2025.102451) |
[open-access primary record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12022633/)

**What was tested**

- **25** commercial avocado-oil brands purchased from U.S. supermarkets and
  two online retailers, with **three bottles per brand (75 bottles)**.
- The products included extra-virgin and refined oils. Five declared blend
  products, represented by three bottles each, were used as comparison oils.
- Samples were acquired in two testing periods, 2021 and 2023. The authors
  measured fatty-acid profiles and avocado-associated fatty alcohol esters.

**Exact findings**

- **10 of 25 brands (40%)** met all evaluated fatty-acid standards; **15 of
  25** did not.
- Some extra-virgin-labeled products had fatty alcohol ester patterns that the
  authors said could indicate incorrect extraction-method labeling.
- High- and medium-fault samples aligned statistically with declared blends
  containing canola or safflower oil, making those oils plausible adulterants.
  The study did not directly quantify a named hidden oil in every failing
  brand.

**What this adds**

This provides recent evidence from a research team independent of the UC Davis
market surveys. It strengthens the conclusion that authenticity concerns did
not end with the 2020 sample. However, failing a compositional standard does
not by itself prove intentional fraud, and the anonymized results cannot
support a current warning about a named brand or lot.

### 6. Mayorga-Martinez et al., 2026 - Codex testing and electronic-nose extension

**Source:** Mayorga-Martinez, A. A.; Gonzalez Viejo, C.; Clorio-Carrillo,
J. A.; et al. *Electronic nose and machine learning for rapid, sustainable
detection of avocado oil adulteration.* *Journal of Agriculture and Food
Research* 27 (2026) 102775.
[DOI: 10.1016/j.jafr.2026.102775](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jafr.2026.102775)

**What was tested**

- **25** brands commercialized in the United States, represented by **three
  bottles per brand (75 bottles)** and grouped as extra virgin, grocery-store
  brand, or independent brand.
- Gas chromatography measured fatty acids and phytosterols against the 2024
  Codex avocado-oil ranges. Those laboratory results were then used to train
  an electronic-nose machine-learning classifier.

**Exact findings**

- **10 of 25 brands (40%)** fully complied with both evaluated fatty-acid and
  phytosterol criteria; **15 of 25** did not fully comply.
- The paper reports classification accuracies above **95%** for its electronic-
  nose models. That accuracy applies to classification within this study's
  dataset; it is not a retail-adulteration prevalence estimate.

**Overlap warning**

The sample design, sample count, author group, and 40% compliance result closely
match the 2025 Marin-Obispo study. Accessible records do not establish that
this is a new, independent set of 25 brands. Treat it as an expanded or likely
overlapping analysis, **not** another 25-brand market replication and not an
additional 15 failures to add to the 2025 count.

### 7. Tang et al., 2024 - method confirmation using previously studied oils

**Source:** Tang, F.; Green, H. S.; Wang, S. C.; Hatzakis, E. *Analysis and
authentication of avocado oil by low-field benchtop NMR spectroscopy and
chemometrics.* *Journal of Food Science* 89(7) (2024) 4276-4285.
[DOI: 10.1111/1750-3841.17142](https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.17142) |
[PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38837399/)

The study analyzed **81 oil samples** across avocado and comparison oils, plus
controlled binary blends. It successfully reidentified the three avocado-oil
samples previously shown to be mislabeled soybean oil. Because the commercial
samples came from earlier Green and Wang work, this is useful analytical
confirmation but **not a new retail survey or new prevalence estimate**.

### 8. Wannasin and Wang, 2026 - standards can create false positives

**Source:** Wannasin, D.; Wang, S. C. *Cis-Vaccenic Acid as a Compositional
Marker for Detecting High-Oleic Seed Oil Adulteration in Avocado Oil.*
*Journal of the American Oil Chemists' Society* (2026).
[DOI: 10.1002/aocs.70129](https://doi.org/10.1002/aocs.70129)

**What was tested and why it matters**

- The study evaluated **298 authenticated avocado oils** spanning **24
  cultivars** and **13 producing regions**, then performed controlled blending
  experiments with high-oleic seed oils.
- Geographic origin explained **73.7%** of the observed variation in the
  proposed cis-vaccenic-acid marker, while cultivar explained **18.4%**.
- The authors derived a 3.2% lower tolerance bound that encompassed **99.7%**
  of their authenticated oils and showed a strong linear decline when seed
  oils were added in controlled blends.

This does not estimate retail fraud. It demonstrates why natural origin and
cultivar variation must be built into authenticity thresholds. A rigid rule
can falsely label authentic oils, so Food Expose should never convert one
out-of-range chemical value into an automatic fraud claim.

### 9. Rodriguez-Juan et al., 2026 - contrary commercial evidence under the new Codex standard

**Source:** Rodriguez-Juan, E. M.; Lobo-Prieto, A.; Garcia-Gonzalez, D. L.;
et al. *Quality and purity of commercial avocado oils under the new Codex
Alimentarius standard: A comparative analysis with virgin olive oils.*
*Journal of Food Composition and Analysis* 155 (2026) 109221.
[DOI: 10.1016/j.jfca.2026.109221](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2026.109221)

- The study compared commercial avocado oils with virgin olive oils purchased
  from food stores. The accessible publisher record does not expose the exact
  sample count, so no count from a secondary summary is treated as verified
  here.
- The authors report that the avocado oils **largely complied** with the new
  Codex compositional limits, although some failed individual fatty-acid or
  sterol criteria.
- Quality was variable: avocado-oil peroxide values reached **29.5 meq
  O2/kg**, and the avocado oils had lower average oxidative stability than the
  olive oils (**10.7 hours versus 25.6 hours**).

This European commercial sample is important contrary evidence: recent
commercial testing does not uniformly find that most bottled avocado oils
fail purity criteria. It also reinforces the distinction between authenticity
and oxidation/quality. A public secondary summary reports 7 avocado oils and
6 olive oils, but that count is deliberately not adopted as fact until it can
be checked against the full primary paper.

## How the recent studies change the conclusion

- The strongest new independent market evidence is the 2025 25-brand study:
  10 brands met all measured fatty-acid criteria and 15 did not.
- The 2026 electronic-nose paper should not be counted as a separate market
  batch unless the authors confirm that its 25-brand dataset is independent.
- The 2023 TAG-PCA study found 21/25 samples not pure, but it is another study
  from the UC Davis program and is not a national random sample.
- The 2026 processed-food study remains the most recent evidence for foods
  listing avocado oil as the sole edible oil, but its 48/54 result cannot be
  generalized to bottled avocado oil.
- New standards research shows that natural compositional variability can
  create false positives. A laboratory result outside one range is not enough
  by itself to prove fraud or identify an adulterant.

## Regulatory and third-party context

### FDA

**Source:** U.S. Food and Drug Administration, *HFP Priority Research, Data,
and Method Needs.*
[FDA page](https://www.fda.gov/food/science-research-food/hfp-priority-research-data-and-method-needs)

The FDA lists "evaluate the prevalence of economically motivated adulteration
of high value oils, including avocado oils" as a research need. This is useful
context that the agency sees a measurement gap. It is **not** an FDA finding
that a particular prevalence is true, an enforcement action, or a basis for a
product score.

Targeted searches of FDA warning letters, recalls, and import/enforcement
material on 2026-08-19 did not locate an avocado-oil-specific FDA or GAO
prevalence finding suitable for this dossier. A lack of a located record is
not evidence that no action has ever occurred.

### ConsumerLab third-party testing

**Sources:**

- [ConsumerLab's published 2021 testing summary](https://www.consumerlab.com/news/best-avocado-oils/04-17-2021/)
- [ConsumerLab's published methods page](https://www.consumerlab.com/methods/avocado-oil-review/avocado-oil/)

ConsumerLab reports that it purchased and tested **eight** avocado oils in
2021, that **one** appeared to include oil other than avocado oil, and that
another tasted of rotten avocados. Its public methods page describes free
acidity, fatty-acid composition, peroxide-value testing, a repeat-lab check
for a failed criterion, and blinded laboratory/taster identity.

This is useful independent third-party context, but it is not peer reviewed,
the public summary does not provide the underlying data for every product, and
it should **not** establish an app-wide prevalence or a brand-level rule.

## Claims encountered but not verified for app use

| Claim | Status | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "90% of retail avocado oil is adulterated." | **Unverified / misleading** | No reviewed bottled-oil study supports that general market claim. A 2026 processed-food study found 48/54 selected products compositionally inconsistent, but those were chips, mayonnaise, and dressings rather than retail oil bottles. The bottled-oil studies use different samples and endpoints. |
| "82% of avocado oil is adulterated." | **Unverified / misleading** | The widely repeated 82% figure is a combined 2020 quality-or-authenticity communication, not a confirmed-adulteration rate. The paper itself confirms 3 near-100% soybean-oil substitutions. |
| "All avocado oil brands are diluted with seed oils." | **Unverified** | The studies are limited samples and lots. No source reviewed supports a universal brand or category claim. |
| "Any food listing avocado oil is actually made with seed oils." | **Unverified** | The 2026 study examined selected chips, mayonnaise, and dressings that declared avocado oil as the sole edible oil. It cannot be generalized to every food that lists avocado oil. |
| Brand-specific current warnings from the 2020 or 2023 papers. | **Do not use** | The reviewed evidence is dated, lot-specific, and not a current verification program. |

## Safe language for a future, review-approved note

Do not ship this wording yet. If later approved, it must remain study-scoped:

> Independent studies have found quality and authenticity problems in some
> sampled avocado-oil products. Results vary by product and lot; this is not a
> verification of this specific item.

Avoid: "this avocado oil is fake," "most avocado oil is seed oil," "all
avocado oil is adulterated," or a score penalty based only on the words
"avocado oil" in an ingredient list.

2. An independent Opus reviewer checks scope, calculations, and the distinction
